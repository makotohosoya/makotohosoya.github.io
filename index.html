ABOUT

Japan’s Mercury Magnetospheric Orbiter, Mio, departed Earth in October 2018. It gradually changes course as it passes Earth, Venus and Mercury a total of nine times, arriving at Mercury after an eight-year journey.

In this work, touching the screen produces a sound and sends a particle of light from Earth. The particle follows Mio’s actual trajectory, compressing eight years into 8 minutes and 29 seconds. When it reaches Mercury on the screen, the same sound plays again. Touching with several fingers creates a chord.

Eight minutes and 29 seconds is the time it takes a radio signal from Earth to reach Mio on 21 November 2026, the day it arrives at Mercury. That is how far away it is.

Everything takes place on the screen; no signal is sent to the actual spacecraft or to Mercury.

IMAGE & DATA

The screen presents the solar system as seen from above its northern side. The Sun is at the centre, Earth at launch is below centre, and Mercury at arrival is at the upper right. The thin grey line is Mio’s actual trajectory, the blue lines are the orbits of Earth, Venus and Mercury, and the crosses mark the flybys. The trajectory is based on spacecraft orbit data obtained from the NASA/JPL Horizons System, covering approximately 2,954 days from 21 October 2018 until entry into Mercury orbit on 21 November 2026. The planetary orbits and positions and the flyby locations also use actual data. The background depicts the southern sky; the positions and shapes of the stars, Milky Way and Magellanic Clouds are based on observations.

For the work, the view has been rotated and time compressed. The entire diagram is rotated so that Earth at launch is nearest the viewer, and eight years are compressed into 509 seconds (approximately 5.8 days per second). The sizes of the celestial bodies are also exaggerated. At the scale of the screen, Earth and Mercury would each be smaller than one pixel, so Earth is enlarged approximately 850 times and Mercury 2,000 times; the Sun is approximately twice its actual size. Earth faces the Guiana Space Centre, the launch site, and its coastline is based on actual geography. The colours and clouds of Earth, Mercury’s surface and the glow of the Sun, however, are imagined.
